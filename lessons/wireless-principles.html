Wireless principles

How Wi-Fi carries data over radio

  • 1.11

The problem: a shared, invisible medium

A switch port gives each device its own cable. Wi-Fi gives everyone the same air. Every device in range hears every transmission on its channel, so only one can talk at a time, and anyone nearby can listen in.

That shapes everything about wireless. Devices must take turns, nearby networks must stay off each other's frequencies, and traffic must be encrypted because it cannot be kept off a stranger's laptop. Wi-Fi is defined by the IEEE 802.11 family of standards.

Radio frequency basics

Wi-Fi sends data as radio frequency (RF) waves. The frequency is how many times the wave cycles each second, measured in hertz. Wi-Fi uses three bands: 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and, since Wi-Fi 6E, 6 GHz.

Higher frequencies carry more data but travel less far and pass through walls less well. 2.4 GHz reaches further but is crowded: microwave ovens, Bluetooth and the neighbours all use it.

Signals change as they travel. Walls and water (including people) absorb them. Metal and glass reflect them. They refract (bend) through materials of different density, scatter off rough surfaces and diffract around corners. Signal strength is measured in dBm; -30 dBm is very strong and -90 dBm is barely usable.

Channels and why 1, 6 and 11

Each band is cut into channels. In 2.4 GHz the channel centres are only 5 MHz apart, but each transmission is about 20 to 22 MHz wide, so neighbouring channels overlap. Only 1, 6 and 11 do not overlap each other (the plan used in North America).

Two kinds of interference matter:

  • Co-channel interference: two nearby APs on the same channel must share airtime, so both slow down.
  • Adjacent-channel interference: APs on overlapping channels (say 1 and 3) corrupt each other's signals. This is worse.

So neighbouring APs in 2.4 GHz are set to 1, 6 and 11 in a repeating pattern. The 5 GHz band has many more nonoverlapping 20 MHz channels, which is one reason it is preferred where clients support it.

Taking turns: CSMA/CA

A radio cannot hear a collision while it is transmitting, so Wi-Fi is half duplex and uses CSMA/CA (carrier sense multiple access with collision avoidance). A device listens first, and if the channel is busy it waits a random time before trying again. Every unicast frame must be acknowledged; no acknowledgement means the frame is sent again.

Two clients at opposite ends of a cell may both hear the AP but not each other, so both think the channel is free and talk at once. This is the hidden node problem. An optional exchange of RTS (request to send) and CTS (clear to send) frames lets the AP reserve the channel for one of them. All this waiting is why a busy Wi-Fi network delivers far less than its headline data rate.

SSIDs, BSSs and ESSs

The SSID (service set identifier) is the network name users see, such as Office. An AP announces it in beacon frames, several times a second by default.

  1. BSS (basic service set): one AP and its clients. It is identified by the BSSID, the MAC address of the AP's radio. The area it covers is the basic service area, or cell.
  2. ESS (extended service set): several APs sharing the same SSID, joined by a wired network. A client can roam from one AP to the next without changing networks. Cells should overlap by roughly 10 to 15 percent so roaming does not drop.
  3. IBSS (independent BSS, or ad hoc): devices talking directly with no AP.

The wired network behind the APs is called the distribution system. Each SSID is usually mapped to its own VLAN.

Encryption, briefly

Because anyone can capture the frames, wireless traffic must be encrypted. WEP was the first attempt and is broken. WPA, WPA2 and WPA3 replaced it; WPA2 with AES is the common baseline and WPA3 is the current standard. The lesson on securing a WLAN covers how they differ.

Key terms

SSID
The network name an AP advertises in its beacons.
BSSID
The MAC address of an AP radio; it identifies one BSS.
BSS
One AP and the clients associated with it.
ESS
Several APs with the same SSID, linked by a wired network, so clients can roam.
IBSS (ad hoc)
Wireless devices talking directly to each other with no AP.
Nonoverlapping channels
Channels far enough apart not to interfere. In 2.4 GHz: 1, 6 and 11.
Co-channel interference
Nearby radios on the same channel having to share airtime.
CSMA/CA
Listen before transmitting, wait a random time if busy, and require an acknowledgement for each frame.
Beacon
A frame an AP broadcasts regularly to announce its SSID and capabilities.

For the exam

  • 1.11: describe wireless principles: nonoverlapping Wi-Fi channels, SSID, RF, encryption.
  • 2.4 GHz nonoverlapping channels are 1, 6 and 11. 5 GHz has many more nonoverlapping channels.
  • Higher frequency: more bandwidth, shorter range, poorer wall penetration.
  • Wi-Fi is half duplex and uses CSMA/CA; wired Ethernet with collisions used CSMA/CD.
  • Know BSS, BSSID, ESS and IBSS, and that the BSSID is the AP radio's MAC address.
  • Standards: 802.11b/g/n use 2.4 GHz, 802.11a/ac use 5 GHz, 802.11n uses both, 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) uses 2.4 and 5 GHz and Wi-Fi 6E adds 6 GHz.

Common mistakes

  • Putting neighbouring 2.4 GHz APs on channels such as 1, 3 and 5: they overlap and corrupt each other, so throughput collapses even with a strong signal.
  • Turning AP power up to "fix" coverage: cells overlap too much, co-channel interference rises and clients cling to a distant AP.
  • Thinking hiding the SSID is security: it still appears in client probes and other frames, so it only hides the network from casual users.
  • Confusing SSID with BSSID: one SSID can have many BSSIDs, one per AP radio.

Quick check

1. Three APs cover neighbouring areas in the 2.4 GHz band. Which channel plan avoids overlap?

  1. 1, 4 and 8
  2. 1, 6 and 11
  3. 1, 2 and 3
  4. 6, 6 and 6
Show the answer

B. 1, 6 and 11

Only 1, 6 and 11 are far enough apart not to overlap in 2.4 GHz. All on 6 avoids adjacent-channel interference but makes all three share airtime (co-channel interference).

2. What identifies a single BSS?

  1. The SSID
  2. The VLAN ID
  3. The BSSID, the MAC address of the AP radio
  4. The channel number
Show the answer

C. The BSSID, the MAC address of the AP radio

Many APs can advertise the same SSID in an ESS, so the SSID does not pick out one BSS. The BSSID, the radio's MAC address, does.

3. Why does Wi-Fi use CSMA/CA rather than CSMA/CD?

  1. A radio cannot detect a collision while it is transmitting
  2. Wi-Fi is full duplex
  3. Wi-Fi has no MAC addresses
  4. Collisions cannot happen on radio
Show the answer

A. A radio cannot detect a collision while it is transmitting

Collisions do happen on radio, but a transmitting radio cannot hear them. So Wi-Fi avoids them by waiting a random time, and uses acknowledgements to discover lost frames.

4. Compared with 2.4 GHz, what is true of 5 GHz?

  1. Longer range and fewer channels
  2. Shorter range and fewer channels
  3. Longer range and more channels
  4. Shorter range and more nonoverlapping channels
Show the answer

D. Shorter range and more nonoverlapping channels

Higher frequency signals fade sooner and pass through walls less well, but the 5 GHz band offers many more nonoverlapping channels.

5. A user walks between two APs with the same SSID and stays connected. What is this set of APs called?

  1. An IBSS
  2. A BSS
  3. An ESS
  4. A BSSID
Show the answer

C. An ESS

Several APs sharing an SSID over a common wired network form an extended service set, which is what makes roaming possible.
