Multi-area OSPF

Why OSPF is split into areas

  • 3.4Single-area OSPFv2

The problem: one big area

OSPF works by giving every router the same map of the network and letting each one run the shortest-path calculation on it. In one area that map holds every router and every link. When a link flaps anywhere, every router gets the change, recalculates and may churn its routing table. With four routers nobody notices. With hundreds, a flapping link at a branch can keep a core router busy all day.

Areas cut the map into pieces. A router only holds the full map of the areas it belongs to. What it knows about other areas is a list of prefixes with a cost, not the links behind them.

Area 0 and the area border routers

Area 0 is the backbone. Every other area must attach to it, and traffic between two non-backbone areas always passes through area 0. The routers that sit in area 0 and in another area are area border routers (ABRs). In this lab R2 is in area 1 and area 0, R3 in area 0 and area 2, so both are ABRs. R1 and R4 are internal routers of area 1 and area 2.

An ABR advertises summaries: it tells area 1 about every prefix it can reach in area 0 and beyond, and tells area 0 about the prefixes of area 1. Routers inside area 1 never see R3, R4 or the link between them; they see 192.168.4.0/24 as a prefix that R2 can reach at a certain cost.

What the areas are, exactly

An area is a property of an interface, not of a router. The network 10.0.23.0 0.0.0.3 area 0 statement on R2 says: the interface whose address matches this range belongs to area 0. Two routers only become neighbours on a link if both ends put that link in the same area; the area ID travels in every hello. R2 and R3 must both say area 0 for the 10.0.23.0/30 link, and the backbone link is exactly what an ABR needs to earn its title.

If a change moves one end of that link into area 1, the hellos disagree, the adjacency drops, and R2 stops being an ABR. Area 1 is then an island.

Reading the routing table

Routes learned inside your own area are marked O (intra-area). Routes an ABR summarised from another area are marked O IA (inter-area). On R1, everything beyond its link to R2 is O IA. On R2, 192.168.1.0/24 is O because R2 is in area 1 itself, while 192.168.4.0/24 is O IA because it comes from R3's summary. All of them keep administrative distance 110; an intra-area route always wins over an inter-area route to the same prefix, before the metric is compared.

The metric of an O IA route is the cost to reach the ABR plus the cost the ABR advertised, so the far LAN costs more from R1 than from R2 even though both are inter-area.

Building it here

  1. On each router, router ospf 1 and a router-id you chose (1.1.1.1 to 4.4.4.4), so the neighbour tables are readable.
  2. Network statements with the right area on each link: R1 and the R1 to R2 link in area 1; the R2 to R3 link in area 0 on both routers; the R3 to R4 link and R4's LAN in area 2.
  3. passive-interface on the two LAN interfaces: they are still advertised, but no hellos go out to a segment with no routers.

Check it worked

show ip ospf on R2 reports "Number of areas in this router is 2" and "It is an area border router". show ip ospf neighbor on R2 lists R1 and R3. show ip route on R1 shows the backbone links and 192.168.4.0/24 as O IA via 10.0.12.2. Then ping PC4 from PC1: the summaries have to point the right way in both directions for the reply to come back.

See it happen

In the app, these steps play out as packets moving across the lab's map.

  1. Neighbours inside area 1 R1 and R2 send hellos on the 10.0.12.0/30 link. Both say area 1, so they become neighbours and exchange the map of area 1: R1's LAN and this link.
  2. The backbone link R2 and R3 both put the 10.0.23.0/30 link in area 0. R2 now has interfaces in two areas, one of them the backbone, so it is an area border router.
  3. Summaries cross the backbone R2 summarises area 1 into area 0: "192.168.1.0/24, cost 1". R3, the other ABR, passes the summary into area 2, adding its own cost to R2. R4 installs it as O IA.
  4. And back the other way R3 summarises area 2 into area 0 and R2 passes it into area 1. R1 never learns about R4 or the 10.0.34.0/30 link itself, only the prefix and a cost.
  5. Traffic follows the summaries PC1's ping leaves area 1 at R2, crosses the backbone, and enters area 2 at R3. Each router forwards on its own routing table; none of them needs the whole map.
  6. The reply needs the reverse summary R4 reaches 192.168.1.0/24 through the summary R3 gave it. If area 1 were missing from area 0, the reply would be dropped at R4 with no route.

Key terms

Area
A set of interfaces that share one link-state map. Routers in an area run SPF over that map only.
Backbone area
Area 0. Every other area must connect to it; inter-area traffic crosses it.
Area border router (ABR)
A router with interfaces in area 0 and at least one other area. It advertises summaries between them.
Internal router
A router whose interfaces are all in one area.
Summary (inter-area) route
A prefix and cost that an ABR advertises into an area on behalf of another area. Shown as O IA.
Intra-area route
A route to a prefix inside your own area, computed from the full map. Shown as O.

For the exam

  • 3.4: configure and verify single area OSPFv2 (neighbor adjacencies, point-to-point, broadcast, router ID). Multi-area design and the O IA code come up in reading-the-table questions.
  • Neighbours must agree on the area of the shared link, as well as hello and dead timers, subnet and authentication.
  • An ABR has an interface in area 0 and one in another area. Every non-backbone area must touch area 0 (a virtual link is the exception, not on the exam).
  • Route preference inside OSPF: intra-area beats inter-area beats external, before metric. All keep AD 110.
  • Areas are per interface: one router can be in several areas; one interface is in exactly one.

Common mistakes

  • Putting one end of a link in the wrong area: the adjacency never forms, and show ip ospf neighbor is empty on that interface.
  • Attaching area 2 to area 1 without touching area 0: the "ABR" between them has no backbone interface, so no summaries flow and the areas cannot reach each other.
  • Expecting O for everything: a far LAN learned through an ABR is O IA, and that is correct, not a fault.
  • Forgetting the router ID and getting a surprise ID from an interface address that later changes.

Quick check

1. R2 has interfaces in area 1 and area 0. What is R2 called?

  1. An internal router
  2. A backbone router only
  3. An area border router
  4. A designated router
Show the answer

C. An area border router

An ABR has at least one interface in area 0 and one in another area. It generates the summaries that let areas learn about each other.

2. On R1 (area 1 only), how does 192.168.4.0/24 appear in show ip route?

  1. O IA 192.168.4.0/24 [110/...]
  2. O 192.168.4.0/24 [110/...]
  3. S 192.168.4.0/24 [1/0]
  4. It does not appear; areas do not share routes
Show the answer

A. O IA 192.168.4.0/24 [110/...]

A prefix from another area arrives as a summary from the ABR and is marked O IA. Intra-area (O) is only for prefixes inside the router's own area.

3. R2's link to R3 is moved into area 1 while R3 keeps it in area 0. What happens?

  1. Nothing; the area is only cosmetic
  2. The adjacency drops because the hello areas disagree, and R2 stops being an ABR
  3. R3 becomes the ABR instead
  4. Only the metric changes
Show the answer

B. The adjacency drops because the hello areas disagree, and R2 stops being an ABR

Both ends of a link must be in the same area. With the backbone link gone from R2, area 1 has no ABR and learns nothing beyond itself.

4. Area 2 is attached only to area 1, through a router with no area 0 interface. What do routers in area 1 learn about area 2?

  1. Everything, as O IA routes
  2. Everything, as O routes
  3. Nothing: without an area 0 interface that router is not an ABR and sends no summaries
  4. Only a default route
Show the answer

C. Nothing: without an area 0 interface that router is not an ABR and sends no summaries

Summaries are generated by ABRs and must cross the backbone. A non-backbone area attached to another non-backbone area is isolated (unless a virtual link is configured).

5. R2 has an intra-area route and an inter-area route to the same prefix, and the inter-area one has a lower metric. Which is installed?

  1. The inter-area route, lower metric wins
  2. The intra-area route; intra-area always beats inter-area
  3. Both, load-balanced
  4. Neither, OSPF reports a conflict
Show the answer

B. The intra-area route; intra-area always beats inter-area

OSPF prefers intra-area over inter-area over external, and only compares metrics between routes of the same kind.
